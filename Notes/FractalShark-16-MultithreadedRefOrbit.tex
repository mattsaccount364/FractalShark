\section{Multithreaded Reference Orbit Acceleration}
\label{subsec:ref-orbit-mt}

The CPU threaded backends distribute independent, high-latency MPIR operations
within one reference recurrence. MT3 overlaps two squares with a caller-owned
cross product; MT9 splits each of those products among three arithmetic roles.
Medium-reference reuse instead assigns the real and imaginary perturbation
updates to separate workers (\cref{subsec:ref-orbit-reuse-execution}). Iterations
retain their mathematical dependencies. Performance depends on keeping the
handoff cost small relative to the large-integer work.

The \code{MT5} entry point is currently disabled and falls back to \code{MT3};
it is not a distinct active backend.

\subsection{Shared CPU implementation principles}
\label{subsec:ref-orbit-cpu-principles}

\paragraph{Persistent workers and atomic polling.}
Workers are created once for an orbit calculation and reused for its arithmetic
batches. A job does not create a new thread, block on a condition variable, or
require a scheduler wakeup. Atomic mailboxes or per-worker state flags publish
work and completion; waiting loops use \code{\_mm\_pause}. The processor pause
is a spin-wait hint, not a scheduler yield. Keeping a worker active avoids
repeated blocking/wakeup context switches and makes short handoffs practical
when products are large enough to amortize polling. The operating system can
still preempt a thread, and spinning consumes execution resources.

The implementation separates frequently polled fields with cache-line alignment
and padding. Stable job operands and private result buffers prevent a producer
from overwriting values still in use. Legacy MT3 and reuse workers use pointer
mailboxes; MT9 uses a distinct acquire/release state protocol. Their actual
protocols are described separately below rather than assigning the same memory
ordering to all CPU backends.

\paragraph{Arithmetic storage and allocation.}
MPIR temporaries, job descriptors, operand snapshots, and product destinations
are constructed before the repeated arithmetic. MT9 also reserves its largest
split and assembly buffers before starting workers, then varies active lengths
without resizing them. The arithmetic loop consequently avoids repeated
construction of owned buffers and general heap allocations for each job.
Thread-local MPIR allocators service arithmetic scratch requests where installed;
such a request is distinct from an operating-system or general heap allocation.
The bounded allocator recycles its preallocated blocks, while retained reuse
data uses bump-backed storage that may grow. Output vectors, decompression
storage, and compressed or uncompressed reuse entries have separate storage
costs. The allocation claim concerns the recurring arithmetic, not a guarantee
that an entire reference calculation performs no allocations.

\paragraph{Small helpers and fixed roles.}
Small hot-path helpers are inline where practical, and template specialization
keeps backend and arithmetic-role choices out of repeated dispatch. MT9 binds
each worker's square/multiply and high/low/sum role when starting the thread;
its fixed-width variant also binds operand widths once. Dynamic MT9 reads the
new active widths after acquiring each job. These choices reduce call and
dispatch overhead without assuming that every MPIR call is inlined. Inlining,
preallocation, stable ownership, and atomic polling are common performance
principles across the threaded CPU algorithms, including reuse capture and
medium-reference regeneration.

\subsection{MT3 authoritative recurrence and thread roles}
\label{subsec:ref-orbit-mt3}

\code{MT} and \code{MTPeriodicity3} use the same three arithmetic roles;
the latter enables the caller's low-precision derivative and periodicity work.
All MPIR states and mailbox jobs are initialized before the loop, starting
from $z_1=c$. The X worker squares a stable snapshot of the old real coordinate;
the Y worker squares a stable snapshot of the old imaginary coordinate. Each also
extracts its coordinate's low-precision shadow. The caller owns the cross
product, component assembly, sample/compression output, stopping checks, and
optional caller-side reuse capture. Mode-2 and mode-3 authoritative reuse add
the capture worker detailed in \cref{tab:reference-reuse-threads}; that worker
does not compute a fourth recurrence product.

\paragraph{Asynchronous squaring.}
During authoritative orbit evaluation, two worker threads compute the squared
terms \(x_n^2\) and \(y_n^2\) concurrently.  In parallel, the main thread
evaluates the cross term in
\[
  y_{n+1} = 2 x_n y_n + c_y,
\]
performs low-precision periodicity checks, and manages operand reuse and
serialization.  Once the squared terms are returned, the real component update
is completed as
\begin{equation}
  x_{n+1} = x_n^2 - y_n^2 + c_x.
  \label{eq:mt-real-update}
\end{equation}
The products in a batch all read $x_n,y_n$ before those values are overwritten.
The workers return their products before the main thread publishes the new
state.  For $x_n=3/4$, $y_n=1/2$, and $c=0$, the batch returns $9/16$,
$1/4$, and $2x_ny_n=3/4$, giving $z_{n+1}=5/16+3i/4$.
Using the new real component in the imaginary product would instead give
$2(5/16)(1/2)=5/16$, a different recurrence.  Parallelism therefore requires
stable old operands and a join before their buffers can be reused.

\paragraph{MT3 imaginary-square lookahead.}
The main thread forms $y_{n+1}$ from the cross product before waiting for
$x_n^2$. Once the current $y_n^2$ has been consumed into separate storage,
the Y worker starts $y_{n+1}^2$ while the caller waits for $x_n^2$ and finishes
\eqref{eq:mt-real-update}. A local flag records that the next Y-square is
already in flight. The orbit dependency is therefore finer than a whole-iteration
barrier: $y_{n+1}^2$ needs neither $x_n^2$ nor $x_{n+1}$. Stable square inputs and
separate completed-result storage protect that overlap.

The schedule evaluates the same polynomial as the single-threaded factored
path, but its separately rounded squares need not give bitwise-identical
results at finite precision.

\paragraph{Lock-free handoff with prefetch.}
Inter-thread communication is implemented using a minimal lock-free mailbox
(\code{ThreadPtrs<T>}) consisting of atomic \code{In} and \code{Out}
pointers.  The protocol proceeds as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The producer fills stable operand buffers and publishes a work pointer
        by storing it into \code{In}.
  \item The worker spins until it atomically claims the pointer, prefetches the
        work descriptor, executes the required arithmetic, and then
        publishes the same pointer via \code{Out}.
  \item The producer spins until it retrieves the completed pointer from
        \code{Out}, after which the computed results are consumed and the
        buffers may be reused.  A published pointer is a handoff, not permission
        for both threads to mutate the same operands concurrently.
\end{enumerate}
The legacy worker claims \code{In} with an acquire compare/exchange after
relaxed polling. Completion and retrieval use their existing pointer
compare/exchange operations; some stores are relaxed. This protocol is not
the per-worker acquire/release state machine used by MT9. Polling loops use
the processor pause hint described in \cref{subsec:ref-orbit-cpu-principles}.
To reduce cache-miss latency, \code{PrefetchHighPrec} touches MPIR metadata
and limb data using a fixed 64-byte stride.  In authoritative \code{MT3},
the main thread prefetches completed square results; the explicit square-worker
operand-prefetch calls are inactive.  The reuse perturbation workers also
prefetch their operands.  Prefetching can hide some memory latency, but its
benefit depends on the precision and cache traffic.

\paragraph{Publication and shutdown.}
The caller publishes the preceding low-precision sample after the first
iteration, while workers operate on stable current inputs. It retains the
completed current Y-square separately before dispatching lookahead, and waits
for an earlier capture before overwriting a reuse job. Escape, periodicity,
iteration limits, or cancellation can leave a lookahead job outstanding.
Stop sentinels request shutdown after any current job; joins ensure completion
before shared job buffers are destroyed. Worker-local MPIR objects are
destroyed before their thread-local allocator shutdown. The same lifetime
ordering applies to an additional authoritative reuse worker.

\subsection{Precision-dependent single-thread versus multi-thread trade-offs}
\label{subsec:ref-orbit-mt-tradeoff}

The relative effectiveness of single-threaded and multithreaded reference-orbit
evaluation depends strongly on the active precision, which in practice is
closely correlated with zoom level and MPIR limb count.  Consequently, the
implementation supports both execution modes and selects between them
automatically when \code{PerturbationAlg::Auto} is enabled.

At lower precision (small limb counts), overall performance is often dominated
by fixed overheads such as synchronization, atomic mailbox operations, thread
spinning, and cache traffic.  In this regime, a single-threaded MPIR execution
path benefits from tight instruction locality, reduced fencing, and predictable
cache reuse, and may outperform a parallelized approach despite executing all
arithmetic serially.

As precision increases, the cost structure shifts toward throughput-dominated
big-integer arithmetic.  Large limb counts cause MPIR squaring and multiplication
to dominate total runtime, amortizing synchronization overhead and making
concurrent execution increasingly effective.  Overlapping independent
operations, such as the simultaneous computation of \(x_n^2\) and \(y_n^2\),
yields substantial gains, while explicit limb prefetching further mitigates
memory latency.  Beyond a hardware- and workload-dependent threshold,
multithreaded reference-orbit computation can outperform the
single-threaded path in practice, with speedups increasing as precision grows.

The current \code{Auto} implementation estimates precision in bits, including
the minimum precision margin and excluding reuse padding. It selects
\code{STPeriodicity} below $640$ bits, \code{MTPeriodicity3} from $640$ through
$6{,}783$ bits, and \code{MTPeriodicity3\-PerturbMTHigh\-MTMed3} from $6{,}784$
through $16{,}383$ bits. At $16{,}384$ through $32{,}767$ bits it selects
\code{MTPeriodicity9\-Karatsuba\-DynamicPrecision}, without a hardware-concurrency test.
At $32{,}768$ bits and above it selects GPU reference generation when CUDA,
the effective renderer's reference format, and the supported precision capacity
permit it; otherwise it uses dynamic MT9. These are fixed policy thresholds rather
than a hardware benchmark. Eligibility and precision limits are described in
\cref{subsec:ref-orbit-pipeline}.

\paragraph{A simple cost model.}
Let $C_{\mathrm{mul}}$ be one full-precision multiplication's cost,
$C_{\mathrm{sq}}$ a square's cost, $C_{\mathrm{low}}$ the main thread's
reduced-precision work, $C_{\mathrm{join}}$ the handoff and waiting overhead,
and $C_{\mathrm{assemble}}$ the final additions and publication. Let
$C_{\mathrm{form}}$ cover ST's sum, difference, and doubled-operand formation.
The factored ST path must be compared with its actual two products, rather
than a hypothetical serial execution of all three expanded products. Ignoring
memory contention and optional reuse work, the critical paths are approximately
\begin{align}
T_{\mathrm{ST}} &\approx 2C_{\mathrm{mul}}+C_{\mathrm{low}}
                  +C_{\mathrm{form}}+C_{\mathrm{assemble}}, \\
T_{\mathrm{expanded,serial}} &\approx 2C_{\mathrm{sq}}+C_{\mathrm{mul}}
                  +C_{\mathrm{low}}+C_{\mathrm{assemble}}, \\
T_{\mathrm{MT3}} &\approx
  \max(C_{\mathrm{sq}},C_{\mathrm{mul}}+C_{\mathrm{low}})
  +C_{\mathrm{join}}+C_{\mathrm{assemble}}.
\end{align}
The maximum appears because the three product tasks overlap; the final
assembly waits for the slowest.  As an illustrative calculation, costs of
$12$, $10$, $1$, $2$, and $1$ time units for multiplication, square, reduced
work, join, and assembly, with $C_{\mathrm{form}}=1$, give
$T_{\mathrm{ST}}\approx27$, $T_{\mathrm{expanded,serial}}\approx34$, and
$T_{\mathrm{MT3}}\approx16$.
If multiplication and squaring each cost only $0.05$ units but joining still
costs $0.2$, with $0.01$ each for reduced work, formation, and assembly, ST and
MT3 cost $0.13$ and $0.27$. These are examples of the model, not measured
benchmarks. It is a per-batch approximation, not an exact steady-state model
of Y-square lookahead. More cores cannot eliminate the join, and shared memory bandwidth
can make the parallel products slower than isolated ones.

Overall, single-threaded and multithreaded reference-orbit evaluation are
treated as complementary strategies rather than a strict hierarchy: fewer limbs
favor single-thread execution, while increasing precision increasingly favors
multithreaded decomposition, with \code{Auto} providing an adaptive runtime
selection mechanism.

\subsection{MT9 exact split-product backend}
\label{subsec:ref-orbit-mt9}

The nine-thread Karatsuba reference design is inspired by
Fractadyne~\cite{Fractadyne}, specifically its \code{ParSplit} and
\code{split\_mandel\_walk} implementation in \code{backend\_rug.rs}.
Each of the recurrence's three products is decomposed into three integer
products of approximately half the original operand width. Eight persistent
workers and the caller provide the nine arithmetic roles.

\paragraph{Precision distinction from Fractadyne.}
Both implementations reconstruct the split integer products exactly.
Fractadyne additionally uses a derivative-driven adaptive working-precision
schedule: when the schedule lowers precision, its recurrence products,
additions, and subtractions round at that reduced precision. FractalShark's
fixed \code{MTPeriodicity9Karatsuba} backend keeps recurrence addition and
subtraction at the full configured MPIR precision throughout the orbit. Its
derivative remains a reduced-precision periodicity estimate. The dynamic
variant described in \cref{subsec:ref-orbit-dynamic-precision}
also adjusts the arithmetic precision and independently certifies its samples.
In particular,
``half-width'' describes integer multiplication operands, not a reduction of
the recurrence's configured precision. The comparison refers to the inspected
Fractadyne revision identified in the bibliography.

\paragraph{Exact algebra and final truncation.}
Let $\beta=2^w$, where $w$ is the number of usable bits in an MPIR limb, and
let $m$ be the low-part limb count. For the full stored nonnegative input
mantissas, including guard limbs, define
\[
 X=X_H\beta^m+X_L,\qquad Y=Y_H\beta^m+Y_L.
\]
With exact integer products
$A=X_HY_H$, $B=X_LY_L$, and $C=(X_H+X_L)(Y_H+Y_L)$,
\begin{equation}
 XY=A\beta^{2m}+(C-A-B)\beta^m+B.
 \label{eq:mt9-karatsuba}
\end{equation}
The middle term is $X_HY_L+X_LY_H\ge0$; no truncated subtraction or
unaccounted borrow occurs. Squares use the same identity with $X=Y$.
An extra sum limb accommodates $X_H+X_L$, and unequal high/low widths are
handled by zero padding. The complete integer product is assembled before
any output limbs are discarded.

For a nonzero exact floating-point product with normalized limb exponent $e$,
retaining $q$ limbs toward zero gives
\begin{equation}
 |\widehat{xy}-xy|<\beta^{e-q}.
 \label{eq:mt9-local-truncation}
\end{equation}
The destination retains up to \code{\_mp\_prec+1} limbs. Karatsuba introduces
no error before that final truncation. The bound concerns products of the
stored inputs; it does not bound propagated orbit-state error or certify periodicity.
Ordinary \code{mpf\_mul} can clip inputs differently, so exact split assembly
need not be bitwise identical to MT3. Fixed split buffers accommodate zero and
one-limb inputs, including values produced by cancellation during the orbit.
The caller therefore uses the split backend unconditionally. A zero product
is stored in canonical MPIR form with both \code{\_mp\_size} and
\code{\_mp\_exp} equal to zero. Periodicity termination is compiled out in
benchmark mode, while derivative calculations and escape termination remain active.

\paragraph{Worker ownership and handoff.}
Each square has high, low, and sum workers; its high worker assembles the
product after its two followers complete. The caller computes the cross
product's high part, while two workers compute its low and sum parts.
\Cref{tab:mt9-worker-roles} accounts for every arithmetic thread. $X$ and $Y$
are the unsigned mantissas; signs and exponents are restored when the complete
products are installed in their MPIR destinations.

After product completion, the caller forms $y_{n+1}=2x_ny_n+c_y$ and
$x_{n+1}=x_n^2-y_n^2+c_x$ from the same old-state batch. Low-precision
derivative tracking, scheduling, propagation of the state-error enclosure, and conversion
certification all run on the caller. The last three activities are present
only in the dynamic variant; no worker independently chooses a precision.

Iteration zero is handled by a separate prologue, which captures $z_1=c$ and
advances the recurrence without publishing a sample or updating the derivative.
The steady loop begins at index one and publishes the preceding sampled state,
preserving MT3's sampling delay without a per-iteration startup check. Cancellation
is checked before the prologue and at the existing intervals in the steady loop.
All operand snapshots, products, and assembly buffers are sized before workers
start and reused throughout the orbit. Each worker has a separate padded
atomic state with \code{Idle}, \code{Run}, and \code{Stop} values. Release
stores and acquire loads publish jobs and results, with processor pause
instructions while polling. Followers are dispatched before their lead,
preventing a lead from treating the previous Idle state as new completion.
There is at most one outstanding job per worker, so generation counters and
a shared completion counter are unnecessary. Pending arithmetic must finish
before snapshots are overwritten or workers are stopped.
Each worker's MPIR scratch lifetime is scoped inside its thread. On normal
exit, cancellation, or dynamic rejection, outstanding batches complete before
Stop is published; all eight workers are joined before their owned buffers
are destroyed. A rejected dynamic attempt is discarded and an application
exception reports the failure; a caller can explicitly retry with fixed MT9.
The heuristic schedule can therefore affect cost and acceptance, but cannot
bypass the accepted-sample certificate derived next. A fixed retry does not
inherit a certificate from the rejected attempt.

\subsection{Optional dynamic working precision}
\label{subsec:ref-orbit-dynamic-precision}

\code{MTPeriodicity9\-Karatsuba\-DynamicPrecision} is an optional alternative
to fixed MT9. It uses the same exact split-product algebra, with a variable
active limb count for the recurrence. The current \code{Auto} policy selects
the dynamic variant in the MT9 range and when GPU reference generation is
ineligible. Explicitly selecting fixed MT9 disables dynamic precision.
The dynamic variant does not alter the other CPU backends, CUDA reference
generation, or authoritative reuse modes.
The option appears in the GUI as \emph{MT9 + Periodicity + Karatsuba + Dynamic
Precision}. The CLI selects it explicitly with
\code{-{}-perturbation-alg MTPeriodicity9\allowbreak Karatsuba\allowbreak DynamicPrecision};
\code{Auto} can also resolve to this backend. Parser and help use a shared
catalog with compile-time coverage checks over every reference algorithm.

Two mathematical arguments have different roles. Parameter sensitivity predicts
a working precision likely to be economical. An independent enclosure of the
current orbit-state error certifies the conversions actually consumed downstream.
The precision proposal is heuristic; the acceptance condition has a proof under
the arithmetic assumptions stated below. In particular, the proof applies to
any permitted sequence of working precisions, including one selected with an
inaccurate derivative.

\subsubsection{Why derivative growth can permit fewer bits}
\label{subsec:ref-orbit-dynamic-sensitivity}

Let $c_\star$ denote the finite coordinates supplied to reference generation,
treated as an exact complex number, and let $z_n^\star$ denote their exact
mathematical orbit. These coordinates need not equal an unrounded decimal
location originally entered by a user. Differentiating the recurrence gives
\begin{equation}
 d_{n+1}^\star=2z_n^\star d_n^\star+1,
 \qquad d_0^\star=0,\quad d_1^\star=1.
 \label{eq:dynamic-derivative}
\end{equation}
Locally, $z_n^\star(c_\star+\Delta c)-z_n^\star(c_\star)
=d_n^\star\Delta c+O(|\Delta c|^2)$. Thus a large $|d_n^\star|$ magnifies
the displacement corresponding to a fixed fraction of a pixel. A small error
in the orbit can then represent a still smaller displacement in parameter
space. The backward-error viewpoint also appears in earlier perturbation
error analyses~\cite{Knighty2016Accuracy,MandelbrotNotes2018}.

For a step evaluated at $P$ bits, a useful model for its local rounding residual
is $C S_n2^{-P}$, where $C$ accounts for the arithmetic operations and
\begin{equation}
 S_n=\max(|z_n^\star|^2,|c_\star|).
 \label{eq:dynamic-operand-scale}
\end{equation}
The scale concerns the operands. If $(z_n^\star)^2$ nearly cancels
$c_\star$, the small result does not undo rounding already incurred in the
products. Requiring the local parameter-normalized residual to be about
$C2^{-P_{\mathrm{full}}-g}$ motivates the proposal
\begin{equation}
 P_{n+1}^{\mathrm{proposal}}
 =P_{\mathrm{full}}+\log_2 S_n-\log_2|d_{n+1}^\star|+g,
 \qquad g=112.
 \label{eq:dynamic-precision-proposal}
\end{equation}
Here $P_{\mathrm{full}}$ is the configured working precision, including its
existing coordinate-precision margin; $2^{-P_{\mathrm{full}}}$ is a budget
scale, not an exact identity for pixel spacing. The constant $g$ supplies a
safety margin for the proposal, rather than a theorem bounding propagated state error.

The quadratic recurrence supplies explicit conditions for derivative growth.
Complex multiplication preserves magnitudes multiplicatively, so rotation
alone introduces no extra amplification in a Euclidean disk. The triangle
inequalities applied to \eqref{eq:dynamic-derivative} give
\begin{equation}
 \bigl|2|z_n^\star||d_n^\star|-1\bigr|
 \le |d_{n+1}^\star|
 \le 2|z_n^\star||d_n^\star|+1.
 \label{eq:dynamic-derivative-growth}
\end{equation}
For example, $|z_n^\star|\ge1$ and $|d_n^\star|\ge2$ imply
$|d_{n+1}^\star|\ge\tfrac32|d_n^\star|$. If the orbit continues to satisfy
the magnitude condition, the derivative grows at least geometrically.
The condition is sufficient and need not hold along every useful orbit.
Cancellation against the additive $1$ remains possible: at $c_\star=-1/2$,
$z_1^\star=-1/2$, $d_1^\star=1$, and $d_2^\star=0$.
A near-return makes the next derivative close to $1$ when
$|2z_n^\star d_n^\star|$ is small; small $|z_n^\star|$ alone does not
establish that condition.

\subsubsection{Error propagation and the limits of the precision proposal}

Write $\widetilde z_n^\star$ for the working state and
$\varepsilon_n=\widetilde z_n^\star-z_n^\star$ for its error. A complete
computed step has a residual $\rho_n$ defined by
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde z_{n+1}^\star=(\widetilde z_n^\star)^2+c_\star+\rho_n.
 \label{eq:dynamic-local-residual}
\end{equation}
The difference of squares gives two equivalent \emph{exact} identities:
\begin{align}
 \varepsilon_{n+1}
 &=2z_n^\star\varepsilon_n+\varepsilon_n^2+\rho_n \notag\\
 &=2\widetilde z_n^\star\varepsilon_n-\varepsilon_n^2+\rho_n.
 \label{eq:dynamic-exact-error}
\end{align}
There is no omitted Taylor remainder: the complete nonlinear contribution is
the explicit quadratic term. Its magnitude can be bounded at each
step, including near a return to zero.

Where the relevant derivatives are nonzero, define the parameter-normalized
error $q_n=\varepsilon_n/d_n^\star$. Substitution into the first identity gives
\begin{equation}
 q_{n+1}=\left(1-\frac{1}{d_{n+1}^\star}\right)q_n
       +\frac{\varepsilon_n^2+\rho_n}{d_{n+1}^\star}.
 \label{eq:dynamic-normalized-error}
\end{equation}
The transport factor is close to $1$ when $|d_{n+1}^\star|$ is large.
Let $N$ be the final iteration index for the following estimates.
For the \emph{linearized model}, start with zero error at $z_0^\star=0$
and include any initialization error in $\rho_0$. If
$|\rho_{k-1}/d_k^\star|\le\eta$ for $1\le k\le N$ and these derivatives
are nonzero, repeated substitution yields
\begin{equation}
 |q_N|\le\eta\sum_{k=1}^{N}
       \prod_{j=k+1}^{N}\left|1-\frac{1}{d_j^\star}\right|.
 \label{eq:dynamic-linearized-accumulation}
\end{equation}
An empty product equals $1$. Since $|1-u|\le1+|u|\le\exp(|u|)$,
\begin{equation}
 \prod_{j=k+1}^{N}\left|1-\frac{1}{d_j^\star}\right|
 \le\exp\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{N}\frac{1}{|d_j^\star|}\right).
 \label{eq:dynamic-transport-bound}
\end{equation}
Sustained geometric growth makes the reciprocal-derivative sum bounded.
If each transport product is at most $K$, the linearized parameter-normalized error
is at most $KN\eta$. Consequently, a margin $g$ absorbs that factor only
when its size, together with the local arithmetic constants, fits the
budget. The full argument must also account for the quadratic terms.

Equations~\eqref{eq:dynamic-normalized-error}--\eqref{eq:dynamic-transport-bound}
explain why reducing precision can work in a growing-derivative regime.
They also identify the assumptions needed for a predictive bound. The state
transport product $\prod 2z_j^\star$ is not generally a ratio of parameter
derivatives because \eqref{eq:dynamic-derivative} injects $1$ at every step.
At $c_\star=-1/2$, an existing state perturbation is transported from step
$1$ to step $2$ with linear factor $-1$, while $d_2^\star/d_1^\star=0$.
An arbitrary sequence of rounding residuals therefore cannot be identified
with one common parameter displacement throughout the orbit.

\paragraph{The implemented schedule.}
\code{RefOrbitPrecisionSchedule} advances a separate approximate complex
derivative using binary64 components and one shared wide exponent. It proposes
work using the component norm $\|\widetilde d\|_\infty$ and an upper estimate
of the operand scale. For a given complex value, replacing its Euclidean norm
by the component norm asks for at most $\log_2\sqrt2=1/2$ additional bit;
that norm comparison does not bound the derivative tracker's arithmetic error.
The periodicity derivative remains separate.

With $w=\code{GMP\_NUMB\_BITS}$, the tier quantum is
\begin{equation}
 Q=\max\left(w,\;w\left\lfloor\frac{P_{\mathrm{full}}}{64w}\right\rfloor\right).
 \label{eq:dynamic-tier-quantum}
\end{equation}
For a finite proposal $a$, the requested precision is
$\min(P_{\mathrm{full}},Q\lceil\max(w,a)/Q\rceil)$.
The startup step stays at full precision, as do nonfinite proposals.
Later requests can decrease or increase. As an illustrative calculation,
$P_{\mathrm{full}}=16{,}384$, $w=64$, unit operand scale, and derivative norm
$2^{8192}$ give $Q=256$ and $a=8304$, hence a request of $8448$ bits.
These values illustrate tier rounding rather than a measured orbit.
Raising a subsequent request restores arithmetic capacity; the precision change
alone does not restore discarded state or reduce the current state-error enclosure.
Later recurrence updates can contract that enclosure, as described below.

\subsubsection{A schedule-independent enclosure proof}
\label{subsec:ref-orbit-dynamic-enclosure}

Let $E_n\ge|\varepsilon_n|$, let $R_n\ge|\widetilde z_n^\star|$, and let
$\tau_n\ge|\rho_n|$ bound the complete local residual. The second identity in
\eqref{eq:dynamic-exact-error} implies
\begin{equation}
 |\varepsilon_{n+1}|\le2R_nE_n+E_n^2+\tau_n.
 \label{eq:dynamic-disk-inequality}
\end{equation}
Choosing
\begin{equation}
 E_{n+1}=\operatorname{up}(2R_nE_n+E_n^2+\tau_n)
 \label{eq:dynamic-disk-update}
\end{equation}
therefore preserves the enclosure. Here $\operatorname{up}$ means evaluating
the nonnegative bound with outward rounding at each operation. A Euclidean
disk respects complex rotation; independent component boxes can instead
introduce artificial growth under repeated rotations. Disk propagation for
the Mandelbrot polynomial also appears in~\cite{MandelbrotNotes2018}.

\paragraph{Meaning of the propagated bound.}
The radius $E_n$ bounds the error in the current working state, including the
transported effects of earlier residuals. It is neither a measurement of the
unknown error $\varepsilon_n$ nor a running sum of rounding-error magnitudes.
One disk bounds both real and imaginary component errors. Local arithmetic
bounds and outward rounding propagate the entire enclosure; an oracle orbit
is not required for the bound calculation. A separately computed oracle can
instead test whether an implementation respects that enclosure.

The update can amplify or contract the radius. At a computed state
$\widetilde z_n^\star=0$, with no newly introduced residual, every possible
exact state lies within $|z_n^\star|\le E_n$, so squaring maps the disk into a
disk of radius $E_n^2$. For example, $E_n=2^{-100}$ permits a next radius of
$2^{-200}$ when the subsequent arithmetic and coordinate addition are exact.
For a nonzero working state, the linear term $2R_nE_n$ and the new residual
bound $\tau_n$ also contribute. Such contraction follows from the recurrence;
it does not reconstruct the discarded earlier state. A small final radius
therefore does not establish how tight the certificate was at earlier states.

The initialization and precision changes require explicit accounting. The
working coordinates $\widetilde c$ are copied once at full working precision.
Their copy-error norm $C_{\mathrm{copy}}\ge|\widetilde c-c_\star|$ initializes
$E_1$, since the prologue starts from $\widetilde z_1^\star=\widetilde c$.
The same coordinate error contributes to $\tau_n$ on every later addition.
If changing precision clips the state to $\overline z_n^\star$, first set
\[
 \overline E_n=\operatorname{up}
   (E_n+|\overline z_n^\star-\widetilde z_n^\star|_{\mathrm{upper}}).
\]
Apply \eqref{eq:dynamic-disk-update} to the clipped state and enlarged radius.
Componentwise truncation toward zero cannot increase the state magnitude, so
the magnitude upper bound captured before clipping remains valid. Subsequent
uses of $\widetilde z_n^\star,E_n$ refer to that current state and enclosure.

\paragraph{Bounding the complete local residual.}
The implementation uses the actual MPIR limb representation, rather than
assuming each operation has a relative error measured against its result
\cite{GMPFloatManual,MPIR_Library}. Write $\pi=\code{\_mp\_prec}$ for a
destination's internal limb precision, distinct from the bit count $P$.
The estimates consumed by \code{ComputeStep} are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
 \item Split-product assembly is exact over all stored input limbs.
       Final retention of $q$ leading limbs at limb exponent $e$ has the
       error bound \eqref{eq:mt9-local-truncation}; a product shorter than
       the retention capacity has no discarded product tail.
 \item Copying an operand has zero error if its occupied limbs fit the
       destination's $\pi+1$ retained limbs. Otherwise its discarded tail
       is less than $\beta^{e-(\pi+1)}$. The same rule bounds explicit
       state clipping.
 \item For addition/subtraction, let $e_{\max}$ be the larger operand
       exponent and let $e_{\min}$ be the lowest occupied operand-limb
       position. If $e_{\max}-e_{\min}\le\pi$, the occupied span fits and
       the operation is exact. Otherwise $4\beta^{e_{\max}-\pi}$ bounds
       aligned operand tails and final truncation. Cancellation does not
       reduce those operand-tail bounds.
 \item Doubling is exact when the occupied source fits $\pi$ limbs.
       Otherwise $4\beta^{e-\pi}$ conservatively bounds guard-limb clipping.
\end{itemize}
For the real update, sum the two square-product bounds and the following
subtraction and coordinate-addition bounds to obtain $a_n$. For the imaginary
update, sum twice the cross-product bound, the doubling bound, and the
coordinate-addition bound to obtain $b_n$. Then use
\[
 \tau_n=\operatorname{up}\left(\sqrt{a_n^2+b_n^2}+C_{\mathrm{copy}}\right).
\]
Rounding metadata is captured before its operands are overwritten. Each
primitive estimate is zero or a power of two; the four real and three
imaginary terms are accumulated outward before updating the disk.

Nonnegative bounds use normalized binary64 significands with checked
64-bit exponents, separately from the renderer's HDR arithmetic. The bound
operations round outward, including when a small aligned addend is lost.
The implementation assumes IEEE binary64, precise floating-point evaluation,
and the supported x64 square-root rounding behavior. Exponents outside the
supported range and invalid bounds reject an attempt; they cannot silently
turn into zero. These arithmetic hypotheses are part of the certificate.

\paragraph{Enclosure proposition and proof.}
For any permitted sequence of working precisions, suppose that coordinate
copying and state clipping are enclosed as above, every local residual bound
is sound, and every bound operation rounds outward without an undetected
range failure. Then $|\widetilde z_n^\star-z_n^\star|\le E_n$ at every
certified state. The coordinate-copy bound proves the base case. The triangle
inequality preserves it during state clipping. Equation~\eqref{eq:dynamic-disk-inequality}
and outward evaluation prove the induction step. Neither derivative accuracy,
monotonic derivative growth, nor omission of the nonlinear term is needed.
The result is an arithmetic enclosure theorem, conditional on the stated
primitive bounds and floating-point behavior; it is not a claim of formal
verification of the machine code.

\subsubsection{Conversion certificates and rejection}
\label{subsec:ref-orbit-dynamic-certificate}

A small error bound alone does not establish identical stored conversions.
The recurrence itself supplies an exact counterexample to uniform boundary
separation:
\begin{equation}
 c_\star=-\tfrac12+t
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 z_2^\star=-\tfrac14+t^2.
 \label{eq:dynamic-boundary-example}
\end{equation}
A sufficiently small dyadic $t=2^{-m}$ places the exact result arbitrarily
close to the representable boundary $-1/4$. No orbit-independent positive
distance from conversion boundaries follows from the quadratic structure.
The implemented acceptance test measures that distance for each sampled state.

MPIR's conversion to a normalized binary64 significand truncates toward zero
\cite{GMPFloatManual}. For a nonzero working component $v$, let $d$ be the
positive truncated normalized significand and let $s$ be its returned binary
exponent. Its positive-magnitude conversion bin is
\begin{equation}
 [B_0,B_1)=
 [d\,2^s,\operatorname{nextafter}(d,+\infty)\,2^s).
 \label{eq:dynamic-conversion-bin}
\end{equation}
Every exact value of the same sign whose magnitude lies in that bin has the
same normalized conversion. Since the complex error disk bounds each
component error, the strict test
\begin{equation}
 E_n<\min(|v|-B_0,\;B_1-|v|)
 \label{eq:dynamic-conversion-test}
\end{equation}
places the entire component enclosure inside the bin and preserves its sign.
It therefore certifies the conversion. Zero error certifies equality directly;
strict inequalities otherwise conservatively reject inclusive bin endpoints.

For example, with $s=0$, let $d=3/4$ and $v=3/4+2^{-55}$. The bin width is
$2^{-53}$, so the two gaps are $2^{-55}$ and $3\cdot2^{-55}$.
An error radius $2^{-56}$ passes; $2^{-54}$ fails. The comparison concerns
the bin gaps, rather than the working precision alone.

The bin boundaries are exact dyadics. Boundary scratch has $256$ bits;
gap scratch has the full working precision plus two limbs, allowing exact
subtraction even for a gap far below the leading limb. A defensive subtraction
bound is added to $E_n$ before comparison with a lower bound for the gap.
Native subnormal/overflow cases and unsupported HDR exponents reject the
dynamic attempt. For exactly real $c_\star$, the imaginary orbit remains exactly
zero by the recurrence, independently of the real-component error radius;
the implementation preserves that structural exception.

\paragraph{Accepted-sample theorem.}
Under the enclosure proposition's hypotheses and the supported conversion
semantics, every component accepted by the conversion test equals the
conversion of the exact orbit for the finite input coordinates. The proof is
enclosure containment in one conversion bin, with the exact-zero cases above.
Certifying the normalized binary64/exponent pair also certifies the existing
deterministic casts to the renderer's sample types. This theorem holds for
any allowed precision schedule. An approximate derivative can affect the
amount of work or the likelihood of rejection, but cannot invalidate an
accepted sample when the theorem's hypotheses hold.

Each sampled state is certified before it supplies input to the existing
publication, periodicity, or escape pipeline. A dynamic attempt remains private
until completion. An ambiguous conversion or invalid bound rejects the attempt;
the implementation records diagnostics and throws an application exception.
Workers, precision guards, and TLS-owned arithmetic objects unwind, and a
caller may explicitly select fixed MT9 afterward. No automatic full-precision
retry is performed. Cancellation publishes only
the already certified prefix and does not start another attempt.

The certificate applies to samples entering storage/compression. Compression
retains its established error contract. The periodicity test remains a
termination heuristic, and downstream pixel arithmetic has its own errors.
An explicitly requested fixed retry retains fixed MT9's existing numerical
contract; its output does not inherit the dynamic attempt's certificate. Likewise, agreement with
fixed MT9 in regression fixtures is evidence about those fixtures, rather
than a universal identity between two finite-precision algorithms.

\paragraph{Reading the worst checked error budgets.}
The completion and rejection summaries retain each component's tightest
checked certificate across the attempt. They do not substitute the final
computed pipeline state, which can be ahead of the last stored sample.
For a nonzero component with normalized binary exponent $s$, define its full
precision unit as $u_v=2^{s-P_{\mathrm{full}}}$. At a checked boundary $b$,
let $G_b$ be the gap lower bound and let $E_{\mathrm{cmp},b}$ be the propagated
radius plus the outward-rounded gap-subtraction allowance. The printed pair is
\begin{equation}
 b_{\mathrm{current}}=\log_2(E_{\mathrm{cmp},b}/u_v),
 \qquad b_{\mathrm{max}}=\log_2(G_b/u_v).
 \label{eq:dynamic-diagnostic-budget}
\end{equation}
The difference $b_{\mathrm{max}}-b_{\mathrm{current}}$ is certification
headroom in bits. The stored record maximizes
$E_{\mathrm{cmp},b}/G_b$, with the earliest checked state retained when the
binary64 pressure representations tie. Real and imaginary records can refer
to different states. Bounds and their exponents are retained without copying
orbit states; logarithms are evaluated only when producing the report.

For example,
\begin{lstlisting}
worst_real_error_bits=120.00/123.00 worst_real_error_state_Z=4567
\end{lstlisting}
reports three bits of headroom at $Z_{4567}$: the comparison-error bound is
eight times smaller than its checked limit. The imaginary fields have the
same interpretation. Both values are logarithmic magnitudes in the stated
unit, not counts of incorrect bits. High coordinate precision can produce a
large maximum at an ordinary-magnitude state, while a near-zero sample can
require a much smaller absolute error. There is no single error allowance
that can be spent uniformly across the orbit.

Accepted fast certificates contribute their conservative prefix-derived gap
bounds with no subtraction allowance. An inconclusive fast probe contributes
no record; only the ensuing regular comparisons determine that check's
record. The summary therefore describes the most restrictive bounds actually
checked, rather than an exact minimum over all mathematical boundary distances.
A tight fast bound can still be resolved by the regular certificate.
Certification uses the original strict bound comparison, independently of the
two-decimal display; equality fails, and rounded values can appear equal even
when the comparison passes.

The status \code{not\_checked} with state index zero denotes an absent check.
Supported zero-error checks and structural imaginary zeros are \code{exact}.
The labels \code{zero\_gap}, \code{unsupported\_conversion}, and
\code{invalid\_bound} explain failures without displaying infinities or NaNs.
An affected component's failure supersedes an earlier finite record; a
component skipped after another component rejects retains its earlier record
or remains \code{not\_checked}.

\subsubsection{Allocation, cost, and provenance}

The dynamic specialization allocates its maximum operand, worker, and assembly
capacity once, then publishes active limb lengths with each joined batch.
\code{mpf\_set\_prec\_raw} changes arithmetic capacity without reallocating or
truncating an existing value. Explicit self-assignment clips the state before
workers snapshot it, and its error is added to the disk. A precision guard
restores the original allocation precision before destruction, as required
by the MPIR/GMP interface~\cite{GMPFloatManual}. Workers and all arithmetic
objects are destroyed before allocator TLS shutdown. Fixed MT9 is a separate
compile-time specialization and does not perform the dynamic certification work.

If $C_{\mathrm{step}}(P)$ denotes the arithmetic cost at $P$ bits, the dynamic
attempt costs approximately
$\sum_n C_{\mathrm{step}}(P_n)$ plus certification and scheduling overhead,
instead of $N C_{\mathrm{step}}(P_{\mathrm{full}})$. Reduced limb counts can
save substantial product work in a favorable growth regime. Conservative
enclosures or conversion boundaries can instead reject an attempt, with a
subsequent full fixed retry available by explicit selection. The strategy
therefore offers a workload-dependent trade-off, with no universal speedup
implied by derivative growth.

Fractadyne supplies the immediate precision-schedule model~\cite{Fractadyne}.
An earlier derivative-driven implementation is present in
\code{gcollombet/mandelbrot} at the June 30, 2026 commit
\cite{Collombet2026Precision}; the linked design note was added at the July 1
commit~\cite{Collombet2026PrecisionDesign}. Its target-depth budget and
precision representation differ from FractalShark's. The derivation above
uses the exact normalized-error identity rather than assuming that a
parameter-derivative ratio equals a state-transport product.

Related numerical-analysis work includes conditioning-driven precision in
homotopy path tracking~\cite{Bates2008AdaptivePrecision}, decreasing working
precision in self-adaptive simulation of chaotic differential equations
\cite{QinLiao2023AdaptiveCNS}, and parameter shadowing for families of real
maps~\cite{NusseYorke1988Shadowing}. Those results provide context with
different hypotheses; the sample certificate here follows from the explicit
quadratic enclosure and conversion-bin proof. The cited sources establish
precedents without asserting universal priority for the rendering strategy.
